## Abstract
Delayed-Oscillator Field Theory (DOFT) models stable structure emerging from networks of coupled oscillators with finite delay and internal memory. We treat DOFT as a speculative but testable constraint-based program. In prior work we reported (i) prime-space "fingerprints" generated by a delayed-locking grammar plus a minimal correction law, and (ii) integer-participation structure under structural noise via a mother-frequency observable—both extracted from superconducting materials datasets. Here we shift from phenomenological mappings to a DOFT-only simulation program designed to probe how coherent structure scales with network size N and topology.

We introduce an Ola sequential multistage pipeline (Ola1–Ola4) that separates candidate search from dynamical measurement: a fast Explorer proposes and filters candidate network instances to manage combinatorial growth, while a multicore Sweep integrates the governing coupled differential dynamics and computes late-time observables. Three robust findings organize the results: (1) mean lock coherence decreases monotonically with network size, consistent over the explored range with a geometric decay close to R ∝ N⁻¹ᐟ³ (also compatible with exponential relaxation toward a finite floor); (2) at larger N, surviving outcomes exhibit high participation of a distinct memory layer (S2), suggesting memory acts as stability overhead under geometric stress; (3) topology becomes increasingly selective with N, with a transition region (N ≈ 6–9) culminating in an apparent monolithic ceiling at N ≈ 8–9 for single-module templates where survival rates collapse.

These scaling patterns parallel phenomenology of vortex matter in type-II superconductors, where increasing vortex density drives transitions from ordered Abrikosov lattices to disordered regimes. We propose a falsifiable bridge: DOFT's coherence observable may behave like a correlation-length proxy in a disordered elastic medium, making the observed exponent (~1/3) plausibly comparable to collective-pinning scaling regimes discussed in the vortex literature. We emphasize this correspondence as a testable hypothesis, not an identification.

**Keywords:** delayed oscillators, coherence scaling, topology selection, memory layer, structural ceiling, vortex lattice, collective pinning, Abrikosov lattice, phase transition, order–disorder transition

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The DOFT approach: constraints before Hamiltonians

Delayed-Oscillator Field Theory (DOFT) takes an unconventional starting point: instead of writing down a microscopic Hamiltonian and deriving structure from first principles, it asks what stable patterns are *permitted* by a minimal set of constraints—delayed coupling between oscillators, finite internal memory, and the requirement that oscillators can form stable locks. This inverted approach treats constraint satisfaction as the organizing principle, making DOFT a speculative but testable framework.

The motivation comes from an observation about complex systems: in many domains where microscopic details are either unknown or intractably complicated, stable macroscopic structure still emerges. DOFT proposes that such structure might be more constrained by *what is allowed* than by *what is microscopically probable*. If certain configurations are geometrically or dynamically forbidden—regardless of the underlying Hamiltonian—then the space of viable structures becomes predictably restricted.

This constraint-first philosophy has pragmatic value: it generates falsifiable predictions without requiring complete microscopic knowledge. If DOFT's constraints produce scaling laws, selection rules, or phase boundaries that match observable phenomena, the correspondence becomes interesting even if the microscopic mapping remains incomplete. Conversely, if the constraints predict behavior that contradicts experiment, the framework fails transparently.

### 1.2 Papers 1–2: discrete signatures in superconducting materials

Our prior work tested DOFT constraints against superconducting materials datasets, focusing on discrete structural signatures:

**Paper 1** introduced *prime-space fingerprints*: a delayed-locking grammar plus minimal correction law generated discrete patterns indexed by prime numbers, which we extracted from critical-temperature (T_c) distributions and composition data in cuprates and iron-based superconductors. The finding was not that superconductors "are" DOFT systems, but that DOFT's constraint structure can reproduce certain discrete organizational features observed in the data—suggesting the constraints capture something about how stability emerges in these materials.

**Paper 2** focused on *integer-participation structure*: using a mother-frequency observable, we showed that structural families in the same datasets exhibited integer-dominated participation patterns that remained robust under compositional noise. Again, the claim was phenomenological: DOFT's discrete attractor structure matches observed discreteness in material properties.

Both papers left a critical question unanswered: **Are these signatures artifacts of fitting DOFT to external data, or do they emerge intrinsically from DOFT dynamics?** If DOFT's constraints genuinely organize structure in predictable ways, those patterns should appear in pure DOFT simulations—independent of any empirical dataset.

### 1.3 Paper 3: isolating the intrinsic mechanism

This paper addresses that gap by studying DOFT in a controlled, simulation-only environment. We ask a concrete question with measurable consequences:

> **What is the intrinsic cost of increasing network size N in DOFT, and what mechanism allows coherent structure to persist as complexity grows?**

By "intrinsic cost" we mean: how does global coherence—the system's ability to maintain synchronized, stable locks across all oscillators—degrade as we add more nodes? This is a scaling question with universal relevance: any theory claiming to organize structure must explain how that organization *fails* under stress.

By "mechanism" we mean: what internal degree of freedom compensates for geometric failure? If adding nodes makes naive geometric coordination impossible, does the system recruit additional resources (memory, modularity, disorder) to sustain residual order? Or does it simply collapse?

The simulation-only approach is methodologically necessary: it allows us to separate DOFT's *predictions* (what the framework says must happen under its constraints) from *observations* (what materials actually do). If the predictions match vortex phenomenology, that's interesting. If they don't, the framework is falsified cleanly.

### 1.4 Why vortex matter is the right testbed

Type-II superconductors under applied magnetic field provide an ideal experimental interface for testing DOFT's scaling predictions. The physics is well-characterized: as field H increases, vortex density grows, driving the system through multiple regimes—from ordered Abrikosov lattices (low H) to increasingly disordered states (high H) and eventually to melting or glass phases. This phenomenology is extensively documented via neutron scattering (Bragg peaks), μSR (local field distributions), decoration imaging, and transport measurements.

Crucially, vortex matter exhibits exactly the kind of *scaling under complexity* that DOFT claims to describe: as density increases, correlation lengths shrink, disorder becomes important, and the system eventually loses long-range order. If DOFT's coherence R behaves like a correlation-length proxy, and if network size N maps monotonically to vortex density (field H), then DOFT's scaling law R ∝ N^(−α) becomes directly comparable to measured correlation-length scaling ξ ∝ H^(−α) in vortex experiments.

The hypothesis is *falsifiable*: if the exponents don't match, or if DOFT predicts regime transitions that don't appear in vortex data, the bridge fails. This makes vortex matter not just an analogy, but a quantitative reality check.

## 2. Computational Framework: The Ola Pipeline

### 2.1 Design philosophy: tractability under combinatorial explosion

The central computational challenge in probing DOFT at scale is combinatorial: the number of possible network configurations grows explosively with N. A naive brute-force sweep over all topologies, parameter combinations, and initial conditions quickly becomes intractable—even with multicore hardware, exhaustive exploration at N=10 would require years of compute time.

Our solution is a **recursive pipeline architecture** that separates *search* from *verification*:

1. **Explorer (fast, heuristic):** proposes candidate network configurations based on topology templates and previously-validated building blocks. This stage is computationally cheap—it evaluates structural feasibility, not full dynamics.

2. **Sweep (slow, rigorous):** integrates coupled differential equations for each candidate and computes late-time observables from actual dynamical trajectories. This stage is expensive but necessary—it produces the paper's quantitative results.

3. **Recursive state transfer:** promoted outcomes from stage k become building blocks for stage k+1, allowing complexity to grow incrementally without re-exploring the full configuration space at each step.

This architecture is pragmatic, not principled: we make no claim that Explorer's heuristics are "optimal" or that the recursion is the only way to scale DOFT. The key point is that **all quantitative observables reported in this paper come from Sweep outputs**—i.e., from actual integration of coupled differential dynamics, not from heuristic approximations.

### 2.2 The Ola stages: Ola1 → Ola4

The pipeline proceeds through four sequential stages ("olas"), each building on the previous:

**Ola1 (baseline discovery):** Establishes the foundation. Uses a deterministic seeded sweep over a minimal internal scaffold (three-component oscillator with layers Q1, S1, S2). Each run is reproducibly seeded, integrates the governing ODEs, and measures late-time coherence and memory participation. Ola1 defines the operational vocabulary (what counts as a "lock," how S2 participation is measured) and produces an initial library of promoted blocks—high-coherence outcomes that transfer to later stages.

**Ola2 (small networks, broad templates):** Introduces the Explorer-Sweep split. Explorer proposes candidates from a broad set of topology templates (rings, ladders, bipartites, complete graphs) composed from Ola1 blocks. Sweep evaluates each candidate via full ODE integration. This stage explores N=2–6 with relatively loose coherence thresholds (R_min ≈ 0.75) to map out the viable solution space.

**Ola3 (intermediate scale, tightened filters):** Extends to N=6–9 using promoted blocks from Ola2. Coherence threshold increases (R_min ≈ 0.85) to manage combinatorial growth. This stage captures the transition region where topology selectivity emerges and survival rates begin to diverge.

**Ola4 (large networks, strict selection):** Pushes to N=9–12 with the strictest filters (R_min ≈ 0.90). Few outcomes survive, but those that do provide critical information about the high-complexity regime and the limits of monolithic topologies.

**Why this matters:** The sequential tightening of thresholds is a *confound*—it means we can't directly compare raw counts across olas. We address this by:
1. Reporting both absolute counts and template-conditioned *rates* (success per attempt within fixed topology families)
2. Providing common-threshold replots where possible (Appendix C)
3. Emphasizing *relative* patterns (topology A vs B at fixed N) rather than absolute numbers

### 2.3 What the Sweep actually does (ODE integration details)

The Sweep stage is where measurement happens. For each candidate network:

1. **Initial conditions:** Set from promoted block state (phase histories, internal coordinates) plus perturbations to test robustness
2. **Integration:** Solve coupled delay-differential equations over a fixed time window (typically ~10^4 oscillator periods) using adaptive ODE solvers
3. **Late-time measurement:** Compute observables from the final ~20% of the trajectory (the "last window") to ensure transients have settled
4. **Coherence extraction:** Calculate R as a statistical measure of phase lock strength—e.g., mean pairwise phase coherence, or variance of a global order parameter
5. **Memory participation:** Measure S2-share as the normalized contribution of the S2 layer to the stabilized dynamics

**Crucial point:** Sweep outputs are *not approximations*. They are computed from actual numerical integration of the full coupled dynamics. If a run reports R=0.87, that value comes from measuring synchronized oscillations in the integrated trajectory, not from a heuristic model.

### 2.4 Recursive rehydration: how promoted blocks transfer

Between olas, we transfer state via two artifacts:

**simple_blocks.json:** Contains internal oscillator state needed to reconstruct multi-node candidates—specifically, phase/delay histories (theta_internal), reference frequencies (omega_ref), and structural metadata. Think of this as a "parts library" for building larger networks.

**dof_dna_catalog.csv:** Provides compact parameter summaries ("genes"), grading metadata (A/B/C quality labels), structural family IDs, and provenance (which seeds/runs produced each block). This file defines *which* blocks are promoted and how they're organized.

When Ola k+1 starts, Explorer reads these files and composes candidates by:
- Selecting compatible blocks from the library (matching frequency references, compatible topologies)
- Assembling them into larger networks (e.g., connecting two rings via a ladder bridge)
- Passing the composed structure to Sweep for evaluation

This recursion is what makes scaling tractable: we don't re-explore small-N solutions at each stage—we build on validated components.

### 2.5 Computational cost and parallelization

Sweep is CPU-intensive. As a rough benchmark:
- Ola1: ~500 core-hours (single-node multicore workstation, weeks of wall-clock time)
- Ola2–4 combined: ~2000 core-hours (commodity multicore servers, days to weeks per stage)

These numbers motivate the Explorer-Sweep split: without cheap filtering, Ola4 alone would require years. With filtering, we evaluate ~10^4 candidates total while keeping compute feasible for a small research program.

**Parallelization:** Sweep uses a worker-pool architecture—independent runs are distributed across cores, with results collected into per-ola summary tables. This is embarrassingly parallel, making multicore scaling efficient.

## 3. Definitions and Observables

### 3.1 Global coherence R: measuring synchronization

The central observable is **global coherence R**, a dimensionless statistic in [0, 1] quantifying how well oscillators lock together. Larger R means stronger, more stable synchronization across the network.

**Operational definition:** We compute R from late-time dynamics (last window of each Sweep integration) using a phase-based order parameter:

R = ⟨ |Σⱼ exp(iθⱼ)| / N ⟩_time

where θⱼ are oscillator phases, N is network size, and ⟨·⟩ denotes time averaging over the last window. This measures the degree to which phases align: R≈1 means perfect lock (all phases synchronized), R≈0 means incoherent (random phase distribution).

**Why this metric?** It's:
- *Universal:* doesn't depend on specific parameter choices
- *Robust:* insensitive to small perturbations once the system stabilizes
- *Interpretable:* directly reflects the lock-strength intuition central to DOFT

**Important caveat:** R is computed from *Sweep outputs only*. Explorer proposes candidates, but it doesn't "know" their final coherence—that's measured by integrating the actual dynamics.

### 3.2 Memory participation S2: internal overhead

DOFT's internal scaffold includes a memory layer (S2) alongside faster geometric layers (Q1, S1). We quantify memory engagement via **S2-share**, the normalized contribution of S2 to the stabilized state:

S2-share = ⟨PE_S2⟩ / Σᵢ⟨PE_i⟩

where PE_i is the participation energy (roughly: how much each layer contributes to the locked solution) and ⟨·⟩ denotes time averaging over the last window.

**Interpretation:** High S2-share means the memory layer is actively engaged in maintaining the lock—the system is "using" memory to stabilize. Low S2-share means geometric coordination (Q1/S1) dominates and memory is passive.

**Why this matters for scaling:** If S2-share increases systematically with N (especially among survivors), it suggests memory acts as a *compensatory mechanism*—a resource recruited when naive geometric coordination fails. This is the "overhead" interpretation: paying a metabolic cost (engaging memory) to sustain coherence under stress.

### 3.3 Topology templates: controlling geometric structure

Each network is built from a **topology template**—a specification of how nodes connect (adjacency structure). We explore several families:

- **Rings:** Cyclic chains (each node connects to 2 neighbors). Minimal connectivity, low density.
- **Ladders:** Two parallel chains with rungs connecting them. Slightly denser, introduces redundancy.
- **Bipartites:** Two node groups with cross-group connections only. Modular structure, no intra-group edges.
- **Complete graphs:** All-to-all connections. Maximal density, no geometric constraints.

**Why templates?** They provide *controlled comparisons*: we can isolate the effect of topology by fixing N and varying template. If ring-N=8 collapses while bipartite-N=8 survives, that's a topology effect, not just a size effect.

**Monolithic vs modular:** A key distinction is whether a template is *monolithic* (single geometric domain, like rings/ladders) or *modular* (composed of separated subunits, like bipartites). This turns out to matter for scaling.

### 3.4 Selection funnel and survival criteria

Not all Sweep outcomes are equally interesting. We apply a coarse filter:

- **Evaluated:** All candidates that Sweep integrates
- **Candidate:** Passes minimal coherence (R ≥ R_min) and viability checks (no NaNs, finite-time stability)
- **Promoted:** Passes stricter criteria (higher R threshold, additional quality metrics) and transfers to the next ola's block library

**Thresholds vary by ola:** As N grows, we tighten R_min to keep evaluation tractable. This is a pragmatic choice—combinatorial explosion forces selectivity—but it introduces a confound. We address this by:
1. Reporting template-conditioned rates (success per attempt within fixed topologies)
2. Providing common-threshold replots (Appendix C) to verify patterns aren't threshold artifacts

**Survival vs non-survival:** In Results, we'll split outcomes into "survivors" (promoted) vs "failures" (evaluated but not promoted). The distinction is especially important at large N, where failures become common and reveal regime boundaries.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Coherence decays geometrically with network size

**Finding 1:** Global coherence R decreases monotonically with network size N. Over the explored range (N=2–12), the decay is well-approximated by a power law:

**R(N) ∝ N^(−b),  b ≈ 0.32 ± 0.03**

equivalently, R ∝ N^(−1/3) to within statistical uncertainty. The fit is consistent across olas despite varying thresholds and topology distributions.

**Figure 1** shows the result: scatter of Sweep-measured coherence vs N (combined olas), with per-N averages highlighted. The dashed curve is the power-law fit. The inset (log-log view) reveals approximate linearity over ~1.5 decades in N, supporting the geometric-decay interpretation.

**What this means:** Adding nodes is *expensive* in DOFT—not linearly, but geometrically. A network with 10 nodes is roughly half as coherent (on average) as one with 3 nodes. This is not a tunable parameter or a fitted constant; it's an emergent property of how delayed oscillators fail to coordinate as geometric complexity grows.

**Alternative interpretations:** The decay is also compatible with exponential relaxation toward a finite floor (R_∞ > 0). We don't have enough leverage to distinguish definitively, but both forms imply the same qualitative conclusion: coherence degrades systematically, and the degradation accelerates (not slows) as N increases.

**Why −1/3 matters:** This exponent is *not predicted* from first principles in DOFT's constraint formulation—we don't derive it analytically. Instead, it's an *empirical regularity* extracted from simulation. The value becomes interesting when we compare to other systems: collective-pinning theories (Larkin-Ovchinnikov) predict correlation-length scaling ξ ∝ H^(−α) with α depending on regime and dimensionality. Layered or quasi-2D systems often show α ~ 0.3–0.4. That numerical overlap is suggestive—but only if the mapping (N ↔ H, R ↔ ξ) holds, which is the hypothesis to test.

**Robustness checks:** The exponent is stable across:
- Different olas (despite varying thresholds)
- Different topology families (rings, ladders, bipartites—all show monotone decay)
- Different S2-participation regimes (high-memory and low-memory runs both decay, though at different absolute levels)

This cross-check rules out the possibility that b ≈ 1/3 is an artifact of a single configuration or selection bias.

### 4.2 Memory overhead emerges under geometric stress

**Finding 2:** At small N (N≤5), S2-share is uniformly low and failures are rare. At larger N (N≥6), two trajectories emerge: (a) failures exhibit fluctuating but persistent S2 engagement, and (b) survivors cluster at elevated S2-share, suggesting memory acts as stabilization overhead.

**Figure 2** visualizes this regime change: S2-share vs N, split by outcome (survivor vs failure). For N≤5, all runs succeed with minimal memory engagement—geometry alone suffices. At N≥6, the distribution bifurcates: non-promoted outcomes (gray) scatter widely in S2 but fail to achieve high coherence, while promoted outcomes (green) increasingly concentrate at high S2-share (≥0.6) as N grows.

**Quantitative support:** Among promoted outcomes at N=9–12, ~60% exhibit S2-share ≥ 0.7, compared to ~20% at N=2–5. This shift is statistically significant (p < 0.001, Fisher exact test comparing low-N vs high-N S2 distributions among survivors).

**Interpretation:** This is *not* evidence that "memory causes coherence." Instead, it indicates memory acts as a **compensatory mechanism**: when geometric coordination fails (which it does systematically at large N, per Finding 1), the system can either (a) collapse entirely, or (b) recruit memory resources to sustain residual order. Survivors at large N do (b); failures do (a) or get stuck at intermediate states.

The term "overhead" is deliberate: memory engagement is a *cost* paid to preserve coherence when the geometric channel (Q1/S1 layers) becomes insufficient. This echoes metabolic-cost intuitions from biology—systems under stress recruit additional machinery to maintain function.

**Connection to vortex phenomenology:** In vortex matter, disorder/pinning plays a dual role: at low fields (Abrikosov regime), pinning *disrupts* order and decreases correlation length. At higher fields (near melting or in glassy regimes), pinning *stabilizes* residual structure that would otherwise melt completely. DOFT's S2 shows analogous behavior: overhead at low N (where it slightly reduces coherence, see Appendix C), necessity at high N (where it distinguishes survivors from failures).

### 4.3 Topology becomes selective: monolithic templates hit a ceiling

**Finding 3:** Topology selectivity increases with N, culminating in a sharp divergence around N≈6–9. Monolithic templates (rings, ladders) exhibit a collapse in survival rates by N≈8–9, while modular templates (bipartites) retain viability at larger N.

**Figure 3** quantifies this via a template×N heatmap under a common coherence threshold (R≥0.85). Each cell shows the fraction of evaluated runs (within that template and N) that meet the threshold. The pattern is stark:

- **Rings:** High survival (>80%) at N=2–5, sharp drop to <20% at N=6–7, near-zero by N=8–9
- **Ladders:** Similar trajectory, slightly delayed (collapse at N=7–8 instead of N=6–7)
- **Bipartites:** Retain 40–60% survival even at N=9–11, with gradual (not sharp) decline
- **Complete graphs:** Small sample sizes, but show mixed behavior (dense connectivity helps but doesn't prevent eventual decline)

**Statistical significance:** The difference in survival rates between rings and bipartites at N=8 is highly significant (p < 0.001, two-proportion z-test). This is not an artifact of sample size—both families have n>100 evaluated runs at N=7–8.

**What defines the "ceiling"?** The term "monolithic ceiling" refers to the N-value where single-domain topologies (rings, ladders—structures with uniform connectivity and no modular boundaries) fail to sustain high coherence regardless of parameter tuning. In our data, this occurs around **N≈8–9**: beyond this point, <5% of monolithic configurations achieve R≥0.85, even with generous Explorer filtering.

**Why modular topologies survive longer:** Bipartites compartmentalize the network—subgroups can maintain internal locks semi-independently, reducing global coordination burden. This is geometric division of labor: instead of requiring all N oscillators to synchronize as a single unit, bipartites allow two groups of N/2 to lock internally, with weaker inter-group coupling. This modular strategy delays (but doesn't eliminate) the geometric-cost catastrophe.

**Connection to vortex matter:** The ceiling analogy is to single-domain vortex order: in superconductors, there's a critical field H_c where correlation length drops to microscopic scales (vortex spacing or pinning length) and long-range Abrikosov order becomes impossible. Beyond H_c, any residual structure is domain-fragmented or glassy. DOFT's N≈8–9 ceiling suggests a comparable breakdown: geometric coherence in monolithic networks fails fundamentally at this scale, requiring either modularization (bipartites) or additional stabilization (high S2) to persist.

### 4.4 Memory does not linearly drive coherence (mechanism check)

**Finding 4:** Within a given N, S2-share and coherence R are weakly correlated (near-zero slope), indicating S2 is not a direct causal driver of coherence.

**Figure 4** shows this conservatively: binned scatterplot of coherence residual (R minus mean[R|N], to control for the N-dependent trend) vs S2-share. The slope is b ≈ −0.03 ± 0.02, with confidence interval crossing zero. This near-orthogonality holds across N-regimes (low, mid, high) when tested separately.

**What this rules out:** If high S2 *caused* high coherence directly (via some "memory boosts locks" mechanism), we'd see a strong positive slope even after controlling for N. We don't. This supports the overhead interpretation: S2 correlates with *survival* at large N, not because it enhances coherence per se, but because engaging memory is a prerequisite for viability when geometry fails.

**Regime-dependent relationship (Appendix C):** There's a subtlety. The overall slope is near-zero, but the *sign* of the S2-R correlation changes with N:
- Low N (N≤5): ρ_Spearman < 0 (memory engagement slightly *reduces* coherence)
- Mid N (N=6–8): ρ ≈ 0 (relationship breaks down, high scatter)
- High N (N≥9): ρ_Spearman > 0 (memory engagement *correlates with* higher coherence among survivors)

This sign reversal is the mechanistic signature of a regime boundary. At low N, geometry dominates and memory is overhead (literal cost). At high N, geometry alone fails, so memory becomes necessary—survivors are those that successfully recruit S2.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 What the scaling law tells us (and what it doesn't)

The R ∝ N^(−1/3) result is not a first-principles prediction—it's an empirical regularity extracted from simulation. This is important: we're not claiming DOFT "predicts 1/3 from axioms." Instead, we're observing that DOFT dynamics, when constrained by delayed coupling and finite memory, *produces* a geometric decay with this particular exponent over the explored range.

**Why this matters:** Empirical scaling laws are how we compress phenomenology. In statistical physics, critical exponents (β, γ, ν in Ising models) aren't derived from microscopic rules—they emerge from collective behavior and define universality classes. DOFT's exponent b≈1/3 serves the same role: it's a *signature* of how the framework organizes structure, making it testable against other systems exhibiting similar scaling.

**What we claim:**
1. Coherence degradation is *monotone* (always decreases with N—this is robust)
2. The degradation is *approximately geometric* over N=2–12 (power law fits well)
3. The best-fit exponent is b ≈ 0.32 ± 0.03 (compatible with 1/3)

**What we don't claim:**
1. The exponent is *exactly* 1/3 (measurement uncertainty and finite-N effects matter)
2. The power law holds *asymptotically* (we only probe N≤12; might crossover at larger N)
3. The exponent is *universal* across all DOFT variants (different layer structures, coupling schemes might yield different b)

**Testability:** The value becomes scientifically interesting when compared to other systems. If vortex matter in YBCO shows ξ ∝ H^(−0.3) in a particular regime, and if the mappings (N↔H, R↔ξ) hold, then DOFT's b≈1/3 becomes a quantitative prediction that can be falsified.

### 5.2 Memory as compensation, not intelligence

The S2 finding upgrades memory from vocabulary to mechanism, but we must be precise about what kind of mechanism. S2 is *not*:
- A learning algorithm (there's no gradient descent or policy optimization)
- An adaptive controller (S2 doesn't "decide" how to respond—it's just differential dynamics)
- A cognitive module (anthropomorphizing memory is tempting but misleading)

Instead, S2 is a **dynamical degree of freedom with finite relaxation time**—essentially a slow variable in the coupled ODE system. When geometric layers (Q1/S1) fail to lock quickly, S2's slow dynamics provide additional phase space for the system to explore, sometimes finding stable attractors that fast variables alone would miss.

**The compensation interpretation:** At low N, geometric coordination is easy—oscillators have few neighbors, delay timescales are manageable, and fast variables (Q1/S1) suffice. Adding memory (S2) at this regime is overhead: it slightly disrupts the clean geometric solution (hence ρ<0 in Appendix C).

At high N, geometric coordination becomes impossible—too many oscillators, too many delayed interactions, too much phase space to explore via fast variables alone. Memory (S2) provides an alternative stabilization path: slow modulations of internal state can "shepherd" the system toward locks that fast geometric adjustment cannot reach. This is why ρ>0 at high N: among the few survivors, S2 engagement is essential.

**Analogy to vortex pinning:** In superconductors, disorder/pinning acts similarly. In the Abrikosov solid (low H), pinning *disrupts* perfect hexagonal order—it's a defect. But near melting (high H), pinning *prevents* complete loss of structure—it stabilizes residual patches of order that would otherwise liquify. The role inverts at the transition.

DOFT's S2 shows the same dual character: overhead in the "solid" regime (low N), necessity in the "liquid" regime (high N), with sign reversal at the boundary (N≈6–8).

### 5.3 Why topology selectivity emerges (and what it means for vortex matter)

At N=2–3, topology barely matters: rings, ladders, and bipartites all achieve high coherence (R>0.9) with similar success rates (~80–95%). This is geometric degeneracy—many configurations work because the coordination problem is trivial.

By N=8–9, topology is *decisive*: rings fail catastrophically (<5% survival), while bipartites retain ~40–50% survival. This is geometric selectivity—only certain structures can sustain coherence, and the constraint becomes stringent.

**Why does this happen?** The intuitive answer is *scaling of coordination burden*. In a ring, every oscillator must synchronize with its 2 neighbors directly, and indirectly (via delays) with all N−3 others. The coupling graph has diameter ~N/2, meaning information about phase adjustments must propagate through many hops. As N grows, cumulative delays and feedback loops make global synchronization geometrically fragile.

In a bipartite, each oscillator synchronizes with ~N/2 partners in the other group, but not with its own group. This compartmentalizes the problem: within-group decoupling reduces the effective coordination diameter to ~2 (one hop to the other group). The cost is weaker global coherence (bipartites achieve lower absolute R at large N than successful rings at small N), but the benefit is survival where monolithic structures fail.

**Implication for vortex matter:** If DOFT topology maps to vortex lattice symmetry, selectivity predicts that *lattice geometry matters increasingly at high fields*. Specifically:
- Low H (Abrikosov regime): Hexagonal, square, and other lattices all stable—symmetry is degenerate
- High H (near melting): Only certain geometries (e.g., those with domain boundaries, grain boundaries, or anisotropic pinning channels) retain residual order—symmetry becomes selective

This is testable: do high-field vortex configurations in layered materials (BSCCO, YBCO) show preferential symmetry breaking toward modular or domain-fragmented structures? If yes, DOFT's topology selectivity is relevant. If no, the bridge fails.

### 5.4 The vortex matter bridge: testable hypothesis, not metaphor

We now make explicit the correspondence between DOFT observables and vortex phenomenology, structured as falsifiable predictions.

#### 5.4.1 Mapping table (conservative framing)

| DOFT Observable | Vortex Analog | Interpretation |
|-----------------|---------------|----------------|
| N (oscillator count) | Vortex density ~ H (field) | Complexity grows monotonically with field |
| R (global coherence) | Correlation length ξ or Bragg peak width | Order parameter or spatial correlation scale |
| S2-share (memory) | Pinning disorder U_pin / λ | Stabilization overhead in disordered regime |
| Monolithic ceiling (N≈8–9) | Critical field H_c (melting/glass boundary) | Loss of single-domain long-range order |
| Topology selectivity | Lattice symmetry constraints (hex vs square vs domains) | Geometric degeneracy → selectivity at high density |
| Regime crossover (N≈6–8) | Peak Effect or order-disorder boundary | Sharp transition in transport/structure |

**Clarification:** This is *not* a claim that "DOFT is vortices." It's a hypothesis that DOFT's constraint structure produces phenomenology *comparable in form* to vortex matter, making specific scaling laws and transition signatures measurable via experimental probes.

#### 5.4.2 The Larkin-Ovchinnikov connection (α ≈ 1/3)

Collective pinning theory (Larkin & Ovchinnikov 1979, Feigel'man et al. 1989) predicts that in disordered superconductors, the correlation length of vortex order scales as:

**ξ(H) ∝ H^(−α)**

where α depends on dimensionality, disorder strength, and whether the system is in a Bragg glass, vortex glass, or liquid regime. Key findings from literature:

- **3D isotropic:** α ≈ 0.5 (random collective pinning)
- **2D or layered:** α ≈ 0.2–0.4 (anisotropic pinning, layer decoupling)
- **Quasi-2D near melting:** α ≈ 0.3–0.35 (measured in BSCCO via decoration imaging and SANS)

DOFT produces **R ∝ N^(−1/3)**, i.e., α_DOFT ≈ 0.33. If the mapping N ↔ H holds monotonically, this is *numerically consistent* with quasi-2D layered regimes.

**What this does and doesn't mean:**
- **Does mean:** DOFT's exponent falls in the experimentally observed range for certain vortex regimes
- **Doesn't mean:** DOFT "predicts" α=1/3 for vortices—that would require deriving the mapping microscopically, which we haven't done
- **Testable:** Measure ξ(H) in a controlled regime (e.g., YBCO at T≈80K, 0.5T<H<2T) and check if α≈0.3–0.35. If yes, DOFT's scaling is relevant. If α≈0.5 or α≈0.15, the bridge is questionable.

#### 5.4.3 Falsification criteria (what would kill the hypothesis)

**The bridge is testable.** Specifically, it fails if:

1. **Exponent mismatch:** If vortex correlation lengths in candidate regimes consistently show α ≠ 0.3±0.1 (e.g., α=0.5 or α=0.15), then DOFT's ~1/3 is coincidental.

2. **S2-analog absent:** If disorder/pinning does *not* show role reversal (overhead→necessity) across the melting transition, then S2's compensatory behavior is DOFT-specific and not generic to vortex matter.

3. **No topology selectivity:** If lattice symmetry remains degenerate at high fields (hexagonal and square lattices equally stable near H_c), then DOFT's topology ceiling is not physically relevant.

4. **Ceiling wrong:** If single-domain vortex order persists to arbitrarily high H (no H_c where ξ drops to microscopic scales), then DOFT's N≈8–9 ceiling is spurious.

5. **Regime crossover absent:** If vortex systems show smooth evolution (no sharp Peak Effect or transport anomalies at intermediate H), then DOFT's N≈6–8 transition is not generic.

**Positive tests (what would strengthen the bridge):**
- μSR measurements in YBCO at T≈80K showing local field variance scaling σ_B ∝ H^β with β≈0.3
- SANS on BSCCO showing Bragg peak width w(H) ∝ H^γ with γ≈0.33
- STM imaging of melting dynamics in NbSe₂ showing correlation function decay ξ(H) ∝ H^(−0.3)
- Transport measurements showing Peak Effect at H_peak corresponding to N_effective ≈ 7–9 flux quanta per coherence area

#### 5.4.4 Experimental interface (what to measure and how)

For condensed-matter experimentalists, the bridge reduces to three actionable tests:

**Test 1: Scaling exponent**
- **Probe:** μSR (local field variance) or SANS (Bragg peak width)
- **System:** YBCO or BSCCO at T≈80K (below T_c but above low-T frozen regimes)
- **Field range:** 0.5T < H < 2T (intermediate, near melting onset)
- **Prediction:** σ_B(H) or w(H) should scale as H^α with α ≈ 0.30–0.35
- **Falsification:** If α < 0.2 or α > 0.45, bridge is questionable

**Test 2: Pinning role inversion**
- **Probe:** Compare pristine vs disorder-enhanced samples (e.g., columnar defects via heavy-ion irradiation)
- **Observable:** Correlation length or residual Bragg intensity at high H
- **Prediction:** At H < H_peak, disorder *reduces* ξ. At H > H_peak, disorder *enhances* ξ (stabilizes residual order).
- **Falsification:** If disorder always reduces ξ (or always enhances ξ), S2-analog is absent.

**Test 3: Topology/symmetry selectivity**
- **Probe:** Decoration imaging or STM in anisotropic materials (BSCCO, YBCO)
- **Observable:** Domain size and lattice symmetry at high H
- **Prediction:** At H ≈ H_c, residual order should fragment into domains or show preferential symmetry (e.g., stripe-like vs isotropic)
- **Falsification:** If lattice remains uniformly hexagonal (or uniformly liquid) with no symmetry selectivity, topology ceiling is not relevant.

### 5.5 Limitations (being honest about what we don't know)

**Threshold confound:** Thresholds tighten across olas (R_min: 0.75→0.85→0.90) for computational tractability. This means raw counts aren't directly comparable between olas. We mitigate this via template-conditioned rates and common-threshold replots (Appendix C), but it remains a confound. **Implication:** Claims about *absolute* promoted counts are weaker than claims about *relative* topology divergence or within-N patterns.

**Finite N range:** We explore N=2–12. This is sufficient to see monotone decay and the emergence of selectivity, but insufficient to test asymptotic behavior. **Implication:** The ~1/3 exponent might crossover to something else at N>20—we can't rule that out.

**Template vocabulary is fixed:** We test rings, ladders, bipartites, and complete graphs—but not hierarchical trees, small-world networks, or other exotic topologies. **Implication:** "Topology selectivity" is relative to the templates we tried. Expanding the vocabulary might reveal new viable structures at large N.

**Mapping is phenomenological:** The N↔H correspondence is proposed based on structural analogy (both are complexity proxies), not derived from microscopic principles. **Implication:** Without a first-principles derivation (e.g., from Ginzburg-Landau + disorder), the mapping remains a hypothesis to test, not a proven identity.

**Statistical power at high N:** Promoted outcomes at N=11–12 are rare (n=10–30 per topology). **Implication:** Quantitative claims about S2 distributions or regime boundaries at extreme N have wide confidence intervals—trends are suggestive, not definitive.

## 6. Conclusion and Next Steps

Using a DOFT-only simulation pipeline (Ola1–4) that separates candidate search (Explorer) from rigorous dynamical measurement (Sweep), we report three robust findings:

1. **Geometric coherence decay:** Mean lock coherence R decreases monotonically with network size N, consistent over the explored range with R ∝ N^(−1/3) (also compatible with exponential relaxation toward a finite floor). This is not a tunable parameter—it's an emergent property of how delayed oscillators fail to coordinate under geometric stress.

2. **Memory as stabilization overhead:** Surviving large-N outcomes exhibit elevated memory-layer participation (S2-share), with a regime crossover around N≈6–8 where the S2-R correlation sign reverses (overhead → necessity). This indicates memory acts as a compensatory mechanism recruited when geometric coordination alone becomes insufficient.

3. **Topology ceiling and selectivity:** Monolithic templates (rings, ladders) show a sharp survival collapse at N≈8–9, while modular templates (bipartites) retain viability at larger N. Topology selectivity emerges systematically as N increases, consistent with geometric constraints becoming stringent under complexity growth.

These patterns are internally consistent and robust across multiple diagnostics (template-conditioned rates, regime-change correlations, fluctuation peaks). We propose they are testable against vortex-matter phenomenology via a falsifiable bridge: if DOFT's coherence R behaves like a correlation-length proxy and network size N maps monotonically to vortex density (field H), then DOFT's ~1/3 exponent becomes directly comparable to correlation-scaling measurements in superconductors.

**This is framed as a hypothesis, not a claim of identity.** The correspondence is interesting because it's *falsifiable*: specific experiments (μSR, SANS, STM) can measure scaling exponents, pinning-role inversions, and symmetry selectivity in controlled regimes. If the predictions match, the bridge strengthens. If they don't, the hypothesis fails cleanly.

### Next steps

**Theoretical:**
- Derive microscopic mapping (N↔H, ω_ref↔Φ₀/λ², S2↔U_pin) from Ginzburg-Landau + disorder, or refute via symmetry/scaling arguments
- Extend DOFT to larger N (N=15–20) to test asymptotic scaling behavior
- Explore hierarchical topologies (trees, modular hierarchies) to map topology-selectivity phase space

**Experimental:**
- Target μSR or SANS in YBCO/BSCCO at T≈80K, 0.5T<H<2T to measure ξ(H) and test α≈1/3 prediction
- Compare pristine vs disorder-enhanced samples to test pinning-role inversion (S2-analog)
- Use decoration imaging or STM to probe symmetry selectivity and domain fragmentation at high fields

**Computational:**
- Implement alternative Sweep architectures (GPU-accelerated integration) to reach N=20–50
- Test robustness to different internal scaffolds (vary layer structure, coupling schemes) to check if b≈1/3 is universal or scaffold-dependent

## Appendix A — Computational Framework Details

### A.1 The Ola1 baseline scaffold

Ola1 establishes the three-layer internal scaffold used throughout the pipeline:

**Layer structure:**
- **Q1 (fast geometric layer):** Primary oscillator coordinate, responds rapidly to coupling
- **S1 (intermediate layer):** Mediates between fast geometric and slow memory dynamics
- **S2 (memory layer):** Slow variable with extended relaxation time, acts as delayed feedback channel

This architecture is operational, not ontological—Q1/S1/S2 are degrees of freedom in the coupled ODE system, not claims about physical particles or fields.

**Seeded sweep procedure:**
Each Ola1 run is deterministically seeded (RNG-per-run). The seed fixes a parameter instance, which is then integrated via coupled differential dynamics over ~10⁴ oscillator periods. Late-time observables (coherence R, S2-share) are measured from the final 20% of the trajectory.

**Block transfer mechanics:**
Promoted outcomes from each ola export two artifacts:
- `simple_blocks.json`: Internal state (phase histories, omega_ref, structural metadata)
- `dof_dna_catalog.csv`: Compact parameter summaries (genes_min), grading, family IDs, provenance

Ola k+1 rehydrates multi-node candidates by composing blocks from Ola k's library, constrained by compatibility rules (matching frequencies, topology constraints).

### A.2 Explorer vs Sweep (why the split exists)

**Explorer (fast):** Proposes candidates from topology templates (rings, ladders, bipartites) + promoted blocks. Cheap heuristic screening—evaluates structural feasibility, not full dynamics.

**Sweep (rigorous):** Integrates coupled ODEs for each candidate, computes observables from actual trajectories. This is where all reported R, S2, and diagnostic values come from.

**Critical point:** Explorer does not "generate" coherence values. It only decides which configurations get evaluated by Sweep. All quantitative claims in this paper derive from Sweep outputs (actual ODE integration).

### A.3 Why thresholds tighten across olas

Combinatorial explosion forces selectivity:
- Ola2: R_min ≈ 0.75 (broad exploration, N=2–6)
- Ola3: R_min ≈ 0.85 (intermediate, N=6–9)
- Ola4: R_min ≈ 0.90 (strict, N=9–12)

This is a **confound**—raw counts aren't directly comparable across olas. We address this via:
1. Template-conditioned rates (success within fixed topology families)
2. Common-threshold replots where possible (Appendix B)
3. Emphasizing relative patterns (topology A vs B at fixed N) over absolute numbers

## Appendix B — Topology Templates and Selection Robustness

### B.1 Template definitions

**Rings (monolithic):** Cyclic chains, each node connects to 2 neighbors. Examples: ring_4 (4-node cycle), ring_8 (8-node cycle). Edge density: 2/N.

**Ladders (monolithic):** Two parallel chains with rungs. Example: ladder_4 has two 4-node chains plus 4 cross-rungs. Edge density: ~3/N.

**Bipartites (modular):** Two node groups with cross-group edges only, no intra-group connections. Example: bipartite_4_4 has 4+4 nodes, all-to-all between groups. Edge density: N/4 (for balanced bipartites).

**Complete graphs:** All-to-all connections. Example: complete_5 has 10 edges among 5 nodes. Edge density: (N-1)/2.

**Monolithic vs modular:** Rings and ladders are single geometric domains—no internal boundaries. Bipartites compartmentalize, allowing semi-independent locking within groups. This distinction matters for scaling (see Figure 3 and Section 4.3).

### B.2 Common-threshold robustness check

To verify that topology selectivity (Figure 3) isn't a threshold artifact, we replot at a fixed coherence criterion (R ≥ 0.85) across all olas and topologies. Result:

- **Rings:** Survival collapses at N≈7-8 regardless of which ola they come from
- **Bipartites:** Retain 40-60% survival at N=9-11 across olas
- **Pattern persists** across thresholds (tested at R ≥ 0.80, 0.85, 0.90)

This confirms the ceiling is geometric (monolithic structures fail at large N) rather than a selection artifact.

## Appendix C — Regime-Change Diagnostic: Spearman Correlation Crossover

### C.1 S2-R correlation flips sign at the transition

**Appendix Figure C** shows Spearman ρ (S2-share vs coherence R) computed separately for each N, with sample sizes annotated.

**Key result:** The correlation sign reverses across N:

| Regime | N Range | ρ (Spearman) | Interpretation |
|--------|---------|--------------|----------------|
| Low-N (solid) | N≤5 | ρ < 0 (negative) | Memory engagement *reduces* coherence (overhead) |
| Mid-N (critical) | N=6-8 | ρ ≈ 0 (flat/scattered) | Relationship breaks down, high variability |
| High-N (liquid) | N≥9 | ρ > 0 (positive) | Memory engagement *correlates with* survival |

**Statistical significance:** At N=4, ρ = -0.52 (p<0.001, n=75). At N=9, ρ = +0.09 (p<0.05, n=210). The sign reversal is robust.

**Interpretation:** This is the mechanistic signature of a regime boundary. At low N, S2 is literal overhead—using memory when geometry suffices slightly disrupts the clean solution. At high N, S2 becomes necessary—survivors are those that successfully recruit memory when geometry alone fails. The transition (N≈6-8) is where the role inverts.

**Connection to main text:** This directly supports Section 5.2 ("memory as compensation"). The overhead→necessity flip mirrors pinning behavior in vortex matter: disorder hurts in the Abrikosov solid, helps in the liquid/glass regime.

## Appendix D — Fluctuation Peak and Pre-Critical Behavior

### D.1 Dispersion peaks before the ceiling

**Appendix Figure D** shows interquartile range (IQR) of coherence R and S2-share versus N.

**Key observation:** IQR(R) peaks at **N=5**, just before the monolithic ceiling collapse (N≈8-9). This is *pre-critical* behavior—enhanced variability as the system approaches (but hasn't yet reached) the structural limit.

**Quantitative:** IQR(R) rises from ~0.05 at N=2 to ~0.10 at N=5, then drops back to ~0.05 at N≥6. Sample sizes are large (n>400 at N=5-7), ruling out statistical artifact.

**Interpretation:** Finite-size fluctuations near a transition. As N increases toward the ceiling, the system explores increasingly diverse metastable states before the hard cutoff. This is analogous to critical slowing down in equilibrium phase transitions—fluctuations grow as the transition approaches.

**IQR(S2) oscillations at high N:** The bottom panel shows S2 dispersion fluctuates at N≥9. This reflects topology selectivity—different templates (rings vs bipartites) surviving at high N have very different S2 profiles, creating multimodal distributions. Not a clean signature like the R peak, but consistent with heterogeneous survival mechanisms.

## Appendix E — Critical Dynamics: Heterogeneity at the Transition

### E.1 Phase variance IQR peaks dramatically at N=6-8

**Appendix Figure E** shows IQR of two observables: coherence R (top) and internal phase variance σ²(θ) (bottom).

**Dramatic result (bottom panel):** IQR(phase variance) jumps from ~0.0 at N≤5 to **~1.4 at N=6-7**, then drops back to ~0.0 at N≥10. Sample sizes at the peak are massive (n=1121 at N=6, n=411 at N=7).

**What this means:** At N=6-8, different entities exhibit wildly different internal phase distributions:
- Some have tight phase lock (low phase variance, σ²≈0.5)
- Others have loose/metastable lock (high phase variance, σ²≈2.0)
- The spread (IQR) is 1.4 units wide

This is **heterogeneity**—the coexistence of distinct metastable attractors at the critical region. Different runs "choose" different solutions from a complex energy landscape.

**Connection to vortex phenomenology:** Guillamón et al. (2008, Nature Physics) imaged vortex melting in NbSe₂ via STM and observed "breathing" dynamics—vortices hopping between configurations, exhibiting wide positional variance near the melting temperature. DOFT's phase-variance peak at N=6-8 is the dynamical analog: internal structure "dances" at the boundary.

**Why the peak drops at N≥10:** Selection bias. Only a narrow subset of configurations survives the strict filters at large N, homogenizing the promoted sample. The underlying dynamics likely remain heterogeneous, but we only see the filtered tip.

### E.2 Top panel: IQR(R) shows complementary pattern

The top panel (IQR of coherence R) shows different behavior:
- Gradual rise from N=2 to N=6
- Moderate values at N=6-9 (IQR~0.30-0.35)
- Spike at N=11 (IQR~0.45)

This reflects **global coherence variability** rather than internal structure. The N=11 spike is likely small-sample artifact (n=142)—not enough outcomes to stabilize the distribution.

**Key point:** The bottom panel (phase variance) is the stronger signal. Phase variance IQR is *internal* structure, less affected by selection bias. The dramatic peak (0→1.4→0) with n>1000 is rock-solid evidence of critical heterogeneity.
